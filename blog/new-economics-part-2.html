The New Economics for Industry, Government & Education by W. E. Deming (Part II)

January 25, 2026 9 min read
Note

This article is the second part of a two part series summarizing the book. I’d recommend starting with part one if you have not yet read it.

In part one, we explored why, in Deming’s view, modern management is failing.

We now ask: how can we do better? Deming’s answer is a new approach to management, one based on collaboration, systems thinking, science, and human psychology.

The framework, which Deming calls “profound knowledge”, consists of four interrelated, reinforcing parts:

  1. Appreciation for systems
  2. Knowledge of variation
  3. Theory of knowledge
  4. Psychology

Appreciation for systems

The first, and arguably most important, aspect of profound knowledge is appreciation for systems.

A system is anything that:

  1. Is comprised of parts (“components”)
  2. That work together (“interdependent”)
  3. in order to accomplish some objective (an “aim”)

“A system is a network of interdependent components that work together to try to accomplish the aim of the system.”

By this definition, a bowling team, orchestra, and business are all types of systems. The bowling team’s team members work together to win the championship. The orchestra’s musicians collaborate to perform intricate pieces. A business’s employees, suppliers, and distributors work together to create sustainable profit.

Not all systems are effective. The most effective systems embody key characteristics:

The system’s aim benefits all participants

A system’s aim must be carefully chosen to create long-term value for everyone touched by the system. When there is organization-wide agreement on the aim, cooperation is incentivized and the system stands a better chance of succeeding.

“It is important that an aim never be defined in terms of a specific activity or method. It must always relate to a better life for everyone.”

The system’s components work together

Systems succeed when everyone involved directs their efforts towards achievement of the system’s aim. If individual departments or people are more interested in achieving their own individual goals (e.g. a quota or promotion), the system is harmed. Optimizing parts does not optimize the whole.

“The obligation of any component is to contribute its best to the system, not to maximize its own production.”

The system considers the future

Participants in the system must account for the future. This requires some level of prediction.

Managers and employees must constantly scan the environment (technical, social, and economic) to perceive the need for innovation. The best systems direct their efforts strategically; they do not become passive victims of circumstance.

“A company can to some extent govern its own future”

The system is managed

Leadership must have a deep understanding of the interrelationships between the components in a system. They must be able to predict how a proposed change will affect things. In effective systems, leadership is constantly applying course correction to ensure the system remains on target.

“A system will not manage itself. Left to themselves in the Western world, components become selfish, competitive, independent profit centers, and thus destroy the system.”

Knowledge of Variation

It is one thing to recognize a system, and it is another thing entirely to improve it.

Well-intentioned leaders often seek out data to help improve the performance their organizations. It is in this context that they first encounter variation. “Why did we have so many defects last month?” “Why were sales up in September?” “How can we repeat last week’s success?”

These are the right questions to ask. But Deming argues, without statistical understanding of variation, these questions are impossible to answer.

“Variation there will always be…[the question is] what is the variation trying to tell us about a process and about the people who work in it?”

A manager’s role is not to eliminate variation but to understand its causes and predict its behavior. Learning this skill enables managers to remove problems and improve the system.

Types of Variation

To that end, it is important to understand that there two types of variation.

“[To] understand the distinction between common causes of variation and special causes [is] to understand the kind of action to take”

Common cause variation

Common cause variation is the natural result of the system as designed.

Consider, for example, a spike in traffic accidents after a city installs unintelligible road signs. The signage has led to driver confusion and collisions. The accidents are the natural result of the system (and stem from a “common cause”).

“Common causes of variation stay the same day to day, lot to lot.”

When common cause variation is observed, management must examine the system and seek out systemic improvements (e.g. ensure all road signs are clear).

Special cause variation

Special cause variation is “something special,” not part of the set of common causes. When there is a special cause, a manager should learn about problem and develop countermeasures to avoid that problem in the future (if recurrence is even possible).

Consider delayed shipments caused by a natural disaster. Unless the natural disaster is likely to reoccur, any changes to the system would be improper.

Common Mistakes

While consulting, Deming observed that managers frequently made two mistakes when attempting to address variation.

  1. Treating a result as a special cause when it came from common causes
  2. Treating a result as a common cause when it came from a special cause

Consider a manager of a car dealership.

For the past three weeks, they’ve sold 200 cars each month. This month they only sold 150. In a panic, he screams “Who messed up?” and fires some of his staff. He didn’t consider that 150 car sales was not a dip at all. An analysis of the data shows it’s within the normal range of sales that the dealership might expect. This is a type 1 mistake.

A few months later, the dealership sees 400 car sales in a single month. Excitedly, he declares “this is the new norm, guys!” and doubles his staff. The increase in sales was due to a limited time tax rebate that has now expired. This is a type 2 mistake.

When managers make changes without understanding, they interfere with the system and make matters worse. Deming referred to this type of uninformed reaction as tampering.

“The result of tampering is only to increase in the future the production of faulty items and mistakes, and to increase costs – exactly the opposite of what we wish to accomplish.”

Theory of Knowledge

Applying “theory of knowledge” is the antithesis of tampering. When you apply theory, you are using evidence to predict how a system will respond to a proposed change.

“The theory of knowledge helps us to understand that management in any form is prediction.”

This is why Deming discourages copying so-called “best practices.” A manager applying theory will recognize that the conditions that made a practice successful in one context is not necessarily present in all contexts. Imitation does not guarantee success.

To the extent possible, Deming recommends gathering evidence based on experiments to support theories. The model used in the book is the Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle, which emphasizes incremental change and reflection.

“Without theory, there is no learning. Theory is a window into the world. Theory leads to prediction.”

Psychology

The final element of Profound Knowledge is an understanding of people.

Deming rejected the assumption that people are primarily motivated by rewards and punishments. He believed people are born with an “intrinsic motivation” to learn, achieve, and cooperate.

Deming argues that modern systems destroy these qualities through grades, rankings, merit pay, and forced competition.

“A show of appreciation to someone may mean far more to him than monetary reward”

Effective leaders eschew judgement. They create effective, cooperative workers by understanding and satisfying the intrinsic motivations of the people they lead.

Conclusion

Most readers of this series will have some experience with traditional management.

Workers labor under deadline pressure to meet arbitrary quotas and adhere to inefficient processes. When the target isn’t met, the workers are blamed. Management unflinchingly carries on.

These conditions are unsustainable, oppressive, and demoralizing. In New Economics, Deming asks a simple question: why continue to practice methods we know don’t work?

Deming’s system of profound knowledge offers an alternative: a management philosophy guided by systems thinking, appreciation of variation, theory, and respect for human dignity.

In the final book he penned before his passing, Deming gives us an opportunity to transform ourselves, our relationships, and our businesses.

“Once the individual understands the system of profound knowledge, he will apply its principles in every kind of relationship with other people.”
